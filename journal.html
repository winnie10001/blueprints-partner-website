Brand / Field note 01

Why the brand work starts before the logo

5 minute read

A logo can make an unclear idea look finished. It cannot make it clear.

Founders often reach for visual identity because it is visible and concrete. A name, a mark and a colour palette feel like progress. The trouble begins when nobody has decided who the business is for, what it should be known for or why someone should choose it.

Those questions shape everything that follows. They determine whether the name feels right, whether the message sounds specific and whether the visual direction has a job beyond looking polished.

Identity should express a decision, not distract from the absence of one.

That is why our brand engagement starts with discovery and position. The identity comes later, once there is something precise for it to carry. The result is not simply a nicer business. It is a business that is easier to explain, easier to recognise and easier to use consistently.

Operations / Field note 02

What a two-week Chief of Staff pilot should move

6 minute read

The test is not how busy the two weeks feel. It is whether one important priority becomes easier to own.

A short pilot needs a narrow target. “Help me run the business” is too broad to judge. Preparing a launch, making a delayed decision or getting one cross-functional project back under control gives the work a real edge.

The first task is to move the priority out of the founder's head. Decisions, dependencies, owners and next actions become visible. That alone does not create momentum, but it reveals why the work has been stalling.

A useful operating partner reduces the number of things the founder must remember, chase and reconstruct.

By the end of the pilot, the priority should have a clearer path, unresolved decisions should be named and the founder should know whether ongoing support would create enough value to justify it. If the work only produced more meetings, the pilot did not do its job.

Systems / Field note 03

When an AI workflow is worth building

4 minute read

Start with the repeated bottleneck, not the tool.

A workflow is a good candidate for AI when the same information must be gathered, interpreted or reshaped again and again, and the current process is slow enough to matter. Weekly reporting, knowledge retrieval and first-line customer questions can fit that pattern.

The presence of repetition is not enough. The source information needs to be trustworthy, the acceptable margin for error needs to be understood and a person must still own the outcome.

The useful question is not “Where can we add AI?” It is “Which recurring delay is worth removing?”

Sometimes the right answer is a small automation. Sometimes it is a clearer template or a better operating habit. We scope selected AI workflows only after understanding the task, because an impressive build that nobody can maintain is another problem, not a solution.
